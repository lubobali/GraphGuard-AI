# Build the queue around capacity, not the model

**Short answer: Phase 7 should look like a small, honest copy of a real AML case manager, not like an ML dashboard.** Real tools (Oracle, Unit21, Lucinity, Flagright, Hawk, NICE Actimize) all share one design: alerts sit in team queues, the regulatory due date sorts first and the risk score second, alerts roll up into a case per customer, every alert carries plain-language reasons, and every decision goes through a reviewer and into an audit log. Managers look at backlog, SLA breaches and alert-to-SAR conversion, almost never at PR-AUC. So GraphGuard's interface should speak that language: a capacity slider ("you can work 100 alerts a day; here is what you catch and what you miss, against the rules"), reason codes built from the XGBoost model's own exact SHAP values, a ring drawn only from edges *before* the alert, and a banner that says "synthetic data, labels arrive late" on every page. The investigator agent is worth building, but only as a measured add-on: no vendor publishes a checked error rate, so a deterministic claim checker with a published error rate is the one thing in Phase 7 that beats the market. The codebase is closer than it looks: all 1,015,300 validation decisions, with the exact feature vector the model saw, already sit in Postgres. But five things block the build and must be decided first: what one "alert" is (transaction, account or case), one capacity number, a durable read-only copy of the data, room in the ~4 GB memory budget, and whether the demo goes public at all.

Words used below: **precision@k** = of the top k alerts, the share that are really laundering. **SAR** = Suspicious Activity Report, the report a bank files with the authorities. **SLA** = the deadline for working an alert. **SHAP** = a method that splits one model score into "how much each input pushed it up or down". **Disposition** = the investigator's verdict on an alert.

## Real case managers sort by deadline, group by customer, and log every decision

The public material is mostly vendor marketing, but the shape of the product is consistent. In Oracle's case manager, the case list shows owner, created date, due date and score; the **default sort is due date, then priority, then case ID**, and overdue dates turn red ([Oracle ECM 8.1.2.11](https://docs.oracle.com/en/industries/financial-services/ofs-analytical-applications/enterprise-case-management/8.1.2.11.0/ecgug/viewing-case-list.html)). Oracle's alert layer sorts the same way, due date first ([Oracle BD](https://docs.oracle.com/en/industries/financial-services/ofs-analytical-applications/behavior-detection/8.1.2.9.0/bduge/alert-list-display-configuration.html)). In Unit21, alerts live in queues owned by teams, a case belongs to exactly one queue, and queues set automatic deadlines ([Unit21 Case Queues](https://docs.unit21.ai/docs/case-queues)). Flagright sets SLA timers by risk level and flags cases before they breach ([Flagright](https://www.flagright.com/case-management)). The lesson for GraphGuard is uncomfortable but useful: **a queue sorted purely by model score looks strange to a practitioner.** A credible queue shows the score, but sorts by "time left" inside score bands, or at least offers that view.

The second pattern is grouping. Vendors roll many transaction alerts into one case per customer or entity: NICE has a single entity trust score ([Finextra](https://finextra.com/pressarticle/91967/nice-actimize-promises-single-risk-score-with-x-sight-entity-risk)), Lucinity supports bulk merge ([Lucinity](https://lucinity.com/case-management)), and Quantexa is built around resolved entities ([Quantexa Community](https://community.quantexa.com/tag/investigators)). GraphGuard today ranks single transactions, while PLAN.md says "ranked accounts". That gap is the first decision Phase 7 has to make.

The third pattern is the workflow. The standard flow is L1 triage (close or escalate), L2 investigation, a second reviewer ("four-eyes" or "maker-checker"), a SAR draft, filing, and an audit log. Lucinity's audit log **separates human decisions from AI decisions** and supports blind review ([Lucinity](https://lucinity.com/case-management)); Flagright has configurable maker-checker approvals and version-tracked SAR drafts ([Flagright](https://www.flagright.com/case-management)). GraphGuard has a natural hook here: the investigator's disposition *is* the late label from S6.9. If Phase 7 records dispositions, they must enter through the same delayed door (`labels.known_as_of`), never straight into training.

What do investigators actually suffer from? The famous "90-95% of alerts are false positives" figure is everywhere but has **no primary source** anyone could find ([FluxForce, vendor](https://www.fluxforce.ai/statistics/false-positive-rates-transaction-monitoring)), so do not quote it. The strong numbers are about effort and outcomes: a 2024 Bank Policy Institute survey of 15 large banks found **21.41 hours per SAR filed**, more than ten times FinCEN's own estimate ([BPI](https://bpi.com/bpi-survey-finds-fincen-significantly-underestimates-sar-filing-demands/)), and of 640,000+ SARs filed in 2017 only **about 4% led to any law-enforcement follow-up** ([BPI](https://bpi.com/getting-to-effectiveness-report-on-u-s-financial-institution-resources-devoted-to-bsa-aml-sanctions-compliance/)). Time per alert is only known from vendor estimates (20-90 minutes), and one KPI source uses **about 20 alerts per analyst per day** as an illustrative baseline ([Sphinx](https://sphinxhq.com/blog-posts/aml-compliance-kpis-metrics)). By that baseline, the 100 alerts a day assumed by GraphGuard's label simulator is a **team of about five analysts**, which is a nice, concrete line for the demo.

Managers watch operations: backlog by age bucket (over 7, 14, 30 days), SLA breaches, handle time, alerts closed per analyst, and the alert → case → SAR funnel. Backlog growth is called "the single most reliable leading indicator" of a capacity problem ([Sphinx](https://sphinxhq.com/blog-posts/aml-compliance-kpis-metrics)). Vendors rarely show model health to managers ([Lucinity](https://lucinity.com/case-management); [Flagright](https://www.flagright.com/case-management)). That is GraphGuard's opening: an operations view *plus* live model health that incumbents do not show.

## Managers understand counts at a fixed workload, and regulators now ask for proof it works

The research on lay readers is clear: the confusion matrix confuses people. In a CMU study (7 interviews, 102-person survey, 483-person experiment), non-experts struggled with its terms and reading direction; a **flow chart was the most preferred redesign, then a bar chart** ([Shen et al., CSCW 2020](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/hongs/files/CM_CSCW2020.pdf)). Counts ("out of 5,000 alerts, 542 are laundering") work better than percentages, even for computer-science students ([arXiv 2209.08312](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.08312)). The banking world already explains thresholds this way, through above-the-line testing (review what fired: would a higher threshold lose anything?) and below-the-line testing (sample just under the line to find misses) ([Sardine BTL](https://www.sardine.ai/learn/below-the-line-testing); [DataInterview](https://www.datainterview.com/courses/risk-fraud-data-science/aml-scenario-tuning)).

GraphGuard's own numbers fit that language directly. On validation, the top 5,000 alerts hold **542 laundering transactions (precision about 10.8%) and touch 127 of 168 rings; the rules alone find 21 in the same budget (precision 0.42%)**, about 26 times fewer. At the very top, precision is 0.96 at k=50 and 0.95 at k=100, then 0.506 at k=500 (repo figures from `docs/leakage_audit.md` and `docs/analysis/s6.8-rules.json`). A manager understands "at the same workload, 26 times more laundering found" in seconds. The capacity screen should therefore show one sentence driven by the slider, a capacity curve (alerts worked vs share of laundering and rings caught) with the rules curve on the same axes and the 0.1% random line, and a "missed" count next to "caught", which is the below-the-line idea made visible. PR-AUC and ROC belong on a separate "for the validator" tab: at a 0.1% fraud rate ROC flatters the model.

Cost can be shown, but only as labelled assumptions on sliders. The common figures, **$30-70 per alert reviewed** ([DataRobot, vendor](https://docs.datarobot.com/en/more-info/biz-accelerators/money-launder.html)) and US AML analyst pay of roughly **$59k-77k** ([Salary.com](https://www.salary.com/research/salary/offering/aml-investigation-analyst-salary); [Payscale](https://payscale.com/research/US/Job=Anti-Money_Laundering_Analyst/Salary)), turn 5,000 alerts into about 1,700-2,500 analyst hours. Two traps: never show "money caught" in dollars, because the amounts are synthetic, and because `evaluate()`'s cost field currently **adds raw amounts across 15 currencies, including Bitcoin and Yen**, so it is meaningless as money (codebase inventory, section 3). Show counts and rings instead.

### Three layers of truth, drawn differently

Honesty is not decoration here; supervisors ask for it. The new US model-risk guidance says users "benefit from understanding and communicating limitations, monitoring performance… and supplementing model output with complementary analysis" ([SR 26-2](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602a1.pdf)). GraphGuard has a special problem: live labels arrive 1-60 days late, and only 5 labels are known by the end of the window (FINDING-017). The fix is to show three layers with different visual weight. First, **measured offline** (validation, solid line). Second, **estimated live** from scores alone, the way NannyML's CBPE method does it, drawn dashed with "assumes no concept drift", because CBPE breaks exactly when the laundering pattern changes ([NannyML](https://github.com/NannyML/nannyml); [arXiv 2604.15740](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.15740)). Third, **confirmed so far** (matured labels only), with a "% still unlabelled" counter and the note that these labels are biased toward what the champion chose to show investigators. "Unlabelled" never means "clean". One more catch from the codebase: the alert line (priority 0.2259) was set on the same validation window the stream replays, so "exactly 5,000 alerts" is true by construction and must be described that way.

### What regulators expect in 2026

The rules changed this year. On **17 April 2026 the Fed, OCC and FDIC replaced SR 11-7 and the 2021 BSA/AML model-risk statement with SR 26-2** ([Fed](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602.htm)). It is guidance, not law: "non-compliance with this guidance will not result in supervisory criticism", aimed mostly at banks over $30bn. Two details matter for GraphGuard. It **excludes "deterministic rule-based processes"** from the definition of a model, so the rules layer is not a model but XGBoost is. And it **excludes generative and agentic AI** from scope, so the LLM agent falls under general risk management, not model-risk rules ([SR 26-2 attachment](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602a1.pdf)). Its validation pillars, conceptual soundness, outcomes analysis and ongoing monitoring, map one-to-one onto things GraphGuard already has: the leakage contract, the validation evaluation, the sealed test set, the shadow challenger.

In the EU, the AMLA draft guidelines on ongoing monitoring (June 2026, final expected Q4 2026) say monitoring must be "explainable", "regularly tested" and "properly documented", and, most quotably, "**simply having such technology in place is not, on its own, proof that the framework works well**" ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/amla-publishes-draft-guidelines-on-ongoing-monitoring-under-article-26-5-amlr)). The EU AI Act does not list AML monitoring as high-risk (its credit-scoring entry excludes fraud detection), and its high-risk duties now start 2 December 2027 ([AI Act Annex III](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/annex-3); [Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)); that reading is an interpretation, not a ruling. FinCEN's April 2026 proposed rule moves toward "effectiveness" and may credit innovative tools "that demonstrate the effectiveness" of a programme ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/04/FinCEN-Proposes-Rule-to-Modernize-AML-CFT-Program-Requirements)). The Wolfsberg Group (August 2025) asks banks to show investigators "the features that influence the model output" and to keep a mapping from typologies to features ([Wolfsberg Part II](https://wolfsberg-group.org/news/the-wolfsberg-group-publishes-its-second-statement-on-effective-monitoring-for-suspicious-activity)). The common thread across all of them is the word **effectiveness**: caught at a fixed workload, not AUC. The demo should also say plainly that this is a portfolio project, and that "validation" in the ML sense is not independent model validation in the SR 26-2 sense.

## Draw the ring left to right in time, with React Flow, and never show the future

For a ring of about 8 accounts (or an ego network of about 50), speed does not matter; layout control and styling do. A live check of the npm registry on 2026-11-22 gave this picture:

| Cytoscape.js | 3.35.0 | MIT | 139 KB | Expandable 1-2 hop neighbourhood: built-in circle/concentric layouts, graph algorithms |
| Sigma.js + graphology | 4.0.6 | MIT | 97 KB | Only if a whole-network view with thousands of nodes is ever needed |

| KeyLines/ReGraph, Ogma | — | Commercial, quote only | — | Out of scope |

(Sources: [npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/cytoscape), [bundlephobia](https://bundlephobia.com/package/@xyflow/react), [GitHub](https://api.github.com/repos/xyflow/xyflow).) The recommendation is **React Flow plus `@dagrejs/dagre` (MIT) for the ring**, laid out left to right by time. React Flow can animate a dot along an edge with SVG `<animateMotion>`, which gives the "money moving" moment for the demo video with no extra library ([React Flow example](https://reactflow.dev/examples/edges/animating-edges)). Add Cytoscape only if the neighbourhood explorer is in scope; one library is simpler. Avoid two traps: elkjs is EPL-2.0/GPL, not MIT-style, so use dagre unless its routing looks bad; and d3-sankey has had no release since 2019 and cannot draw cycles, which rings contain.

The encoding should follow analyst practice. Feedzai's 2026 IEEE VIS paper on money-laundering graphs lays the trail out as a sequence from the alerted account, groups by amount and time to reduce clutter, and found that **the version with the fewest nodes was not the most useful** to expert analysts ([Feedzai, arXiv 2605.10522](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10522)). So offer a toggle between grouped and detailed, not maximum simplification. Neo4j's AML example colours risk red/amber/yellow and gives executives a simple traffic-light summary rather than a graph ([Neo4j](https://neo4j.com/blog/fraud-detection/combating-money-laundering-graph-data-visualizations/)). Even Quantexa admits "the network UI is too confusing for business users" is a known objection ([Quantexa](https://www.quantexa.com/solutions/internal-fraud/)). For GraphGuard that means: columns for Day 1 / Day 2 / Day 3, the alerted transaction with a thick outline, edge width on a square-root scale of amount (the hops are ordinary amounts, and that sameness *is* the story), a "circle" toggle when the ring is a true round trip, and a table of the same transactions next to the picture for people who dislike graphs and for screen readers.

Two rules come from the project itself, not from vendors. **The drawn graph must contain only edges before the alert** (leakage contract rule 2, ordered by `(timestamp, source_row)` because 9.4% of rows tie on the minute); showing later edges is leakage in the UI, and it needs a test. And **hubs must be capped**: some accounts have more than 100,000 transfers (FINDING-002), so collapse anything past about 15 neighbours into a "+N more" node. Finally, the labelled ring from the patterns file is the answer key, which no bank has on the day. Show it only in a clearly separate "answer key" view, and never give it to the agent. Note that the patterns file also holds test-window rings, and the current test-set guard does not catch that (codebase inventory, section 7), so the ring table should be built in the lakehouse first, with the test part sealed.

## Explain with exact SHAP reason codes first; add the LLM only if it can be measured

The explanation needs no new library. XGBoost's own `predict(pred_contribs=True)` gives **exact TreeSHAP values on CPU**, in log-odds, with one extra bias column, and they sum exactly to the model's raw score ([XGBoost docs](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/prediction.html)). Because `stream.decisions` already stores the 15-value feature vector each decision used, the contributions can be precomputed for the alerts and checked with a simple test: sum of contributions, through a sigmoid, equals the stored score. Three cautions belong in the UI copy. The score is **not a probability**: the model was trained with a class weight of about 1,326, so 0.23 does not mean 23%; show rank or percentile. Correlated features (number sent vs amount sent) split credit, so group them into "reason families" before display (SHAP values add, so summing is valid) and say "contributed", not "caused" ([shap docs](https://shap.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/shap.TreeExplainer.html)). And the direction surprises people: launderers here have *fewer* counterparties than ordinary accounts (FINDING-005).

Turning numbers into sentences has a mature model in credit scoring. US rules require "specific" principal reasons even for complex ML models ([CFPB Circular 2022-03](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/circulars/circular-2022-03-adverse-action-notification-requirements-in-connection-with-credit-decisions-based-on-complex-algorithms/)), and credit-score notices disclose **up to four key factors**. AML has no such rule, but the vendor pattern is the same: a reason code plus a plain-language sentence on every alert ([Hawk](https://hawk.ai/solutions/fraud)). So build a small template table keyed by feature family ("Sent {value} payments in the last 24 h"), show the top three positive contributions filled with the as-of values from the decision row, and show rule hits separately, word for word. This is fully deterministic. On this data it carries the whole "why": rules-only precision at 5,000 is 0.0042, and three of the six rules never fire on validation (FINDING-016), so for almost every alert the rules say nothing.

### The agent: the market's claims are unmeasured, which is the opportunity

Every major vendor now ships an investigation agent that gathers evidence, summarises, recommends and drafts the SAR narrative, with a human approving: Lucinity's Luci, Unit21's Alert/Case/SAR agents, Hawk's AML Investigative Agent (March 2026), SymphonyAI's Sensa Copilot ("up to 70% faster", vendor claim) ([Corsa](https://corsa.finance/compare/best-ai-tools-aml-alert-triage-sar-drafting); [SymphonyAI](https://www.symphonyai.com/resources/blogs/symphonyai-sensa-netreveal-unveils-trailblazing-generative-ai-driven-investigator-assistant)). **None of the time-saving or accuracy claims found are independently measured.** The best-known research system, Co-Investigator AI, was rated by 6 investigators on a 1-5 scale, used an LLM judge with **no reported agreement with humans**, and reports no hallucination rate; its "61% time saved" is a rating, not a measurement ([arXiv 2509.08380](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.08380v1)). The better pattern is AMLBench (November 2026), which scores both the outcome against annotations and the agent's tool-call log, and shows how misleading one number is: in two cases recall was 100% and 94% while **precision was only 15% and 7.6%** ([arXiv 2611.02530](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02530)).

So yes, build it, but in this order of trust. The agent returns structured claims, each with a kind (amount, count, counterparty, time, pattern, reason) and the record IDs it cites. A **deterministic checker re-queries every numeric or relational claim** in SQL; a claim is wrong if it is uncited, cites a record outside the window, or does not match. That gives an unsupported-claim rate with no LLM judge involved. An LLM judge is used only for what SQL cannot check ("is this misleading?"), calibrated against a human-labelled sample, and from a different model family than the generator. The eval set comes from validation alerts only, stratified into true positives, false positives and cold-start cases, plus adversarial cases where the right answer is "insufficient evidence". Report the claim error rate with a 95% confidence interval, and **compare against the template reason codes**: if the LLM does not beat the templates on a defined metric, that is a finding, written up like the GNN result. Singapore's regulator lists automation bias as a named risk ([MAS, October 2026](https://www.mas.gov.sg/-/media/mas-media-library/regulation/guidelines/bd/guidelines-on-artificial-intelligence-risk-management-for-financial-institutions/guidelines-on-artificial-intelligence-risk-management.pdf)), so a small extra test, planting errors in drafts and checking if a reviewer catches them, would be rare and strong. Wolfsberg explicitly endorses LLM "investigator-ready summaries" ([Wolfsberg Part II](https://financialcrime.lu/assets/pdfs/articles/2025/08/20250827%20EN%20Statement%20on%20Effective%20Monitoring%20for%20Suspicious%20Activity%20%C2%A6%20Part%20II%20-%20Transitioning%20to%20Innovation_%5BMD5_F987A8AB6D2B85A2FACE6FDE74F704CD%5D.pdf)); no regulator found bans GenAI in AML. The agent drafts and cites; the human decides; every output is logged with model version, prompt version, cited records and the reviewer's accept/edit/reject.

One caution on the PLAN.md gate "unsupported-claim rate at zero". Zero on a small set is achievable with a checker that blocks bad claims, but say clearly whether zero is measured *before* the checker (the model's real error rate) or *after* (the system's output). Both numbers should be reported; only the first says anything about the LLM.

## A strong demo shows the honesty fast, served as static files on one shared box

No rigorous survey of ML hiring managers exists; what exists is practitioner opinion, and it agrees with itself. Reviewers want a deployed thing to click, an end-to-end repo with tests and CI, evaluation against a baseline, written-down failures and limits, and a README that explains the result fast ([LetsDataScience](https://letsdatascience.com/blog/the-ml-portfolio-that-actually-gets-you-hired-in-2026); [MentorCruise](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/career-roadmap-how-to-build-an-ai-portfolio-that-gets-you-hired/)). Eugene Yan reportedly asks how a system was evaluated and what error analysis was done ([Data Root Labs](https://datarootlabs.com/blog/data-science-projects-for-portfolio)). The counterpoint matters most: "when every candidate can generate a working demo in an afternoon, the demo stops being evidence" ([Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/your-ai-portfolio-isnt-missing-projects-it-s-missing-proof-5d738b74995b)). GraphGuard's rare signals are the leakage contract, the GNN that lost, precision@k and the sealed test set. The interface should put those on screen: above the fold, what this is, that the data is synthetic, the headline number against the rules, and one live element. Show **one honest failure** too, such as a missed ring or a false positive with its explanation. The earlier portfolio research adds that fraud-ML hiring is senior-heavy and leans toward serving and monitoring skills, so Phase 7's UI polish matters less than Phases 5, 6 and 8; keep Phase 7 small.

For the video, a 2026 study of 1,750 B2B demo videos found **30 seconds to 2.5 minutes perform about the same, and under 30 seconds does worse** ([Motion the Agency](https://www.motiontheagency.com/insight/b2b-product-demo-video-study)). Show one workflow, do not open with setup, and assume the video plays muted, so captions carry the story ([Velo](https://usevelo.ai/blog/blog-product-demo-video-length/)). A good two-minute arc: problem plus "synthetic data" card; the stream scoring live; an alert opens and the ring animates; the evidence (capacity curve vs rules, GNN finding); honest limits and links.

### Stack and security

The memory budget decides the stack. GraphGuard already uses **about 4.1 GiB against a ~4 GB budget** (codebase inventory, section 9), so a second always-on Node server is the wrong choice. Next.js 16.4 supports **static export** (`output: 'export'`): plain HTML and JS files, client components fetch data in the browser, and there is no Node process at all ([Next.js static exports](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/guides/static-exports)). That also removes most of Next.js's 2026 security surface. Next.js started a **monthly security release programme in July 2026** after a run of advisories, including critical remote-code-execution bugs in image optimization (October) and `next/og` (September) ([Next.js blog](https://nextjs.org/blog/next-security-release-program.md); [GitHub advisories](https://github.com/vercel/next.js/security/advisories)); the 2025 middleware bypass explicitly does not affect static exports ([Next.js](https://nextjs.org/blog/cve-2025-29927)). The live parts come from one small Python API: FastAPI has **built-in Server-Sent Events since 0.135.0**, with keep-alive pings and resume on reconnect through `Last-Event-ID`, which maps neatly onto decision IDs ([FastAPI SSE](https://fastapi.tiangolo.com/tutorial/server-sent-events/)). Data flows one way (decisions to the browser), so SSE is enough; WebSockets are not needed. Use TanStack Table for the queue, shadcn/ui charts (Recharts 3) for small KPI charts, and React Flow for the ring. Pin every package to a release at least 14 days old, as the repo's supply-chain rule already requires, and run OSV-Scanner on the frontend lockfile in CI.

The public API should be boring on purpose: GET-only, a **new SELECT-only database role** on a demo copy (the only stream role today owns and writes the tables), per-IP rate limits, a cap on open SSE connections (each one holds a socket, so this is the main denial-of-service lever), pagination, strict security headers set at the web server, no secrets in `NEXT_PUBLIC_*` variables, and no admin pages in the public build. Serve replayed validation data, not the live decision path. For accessibility, chart marks need 3:1 contrast and text 4.5:1 (WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.11), risk must never be shown by colour alone, a table must sit behind every chart, and animations must respect `prefers-reduced-motion` ([W3C](https://w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast)). PLAN.md says "same stack as LuBot's frontend", but this session may not read `/srv/lubot-*`, so Lubo has to confirm what that stack is.

## Conclusion

The research changes what Phase 7 is for. It is not mainly a UI phase; it is the phase where the project's evaluation becomes something a non-ML person can check. The capacity slider is the model-risk "outcomes analysis" made visible, the three-layer performance strip is the honest answer to delayed labels, and the as-of ring is the leakage contract applied to pixels. The most valuable single artifact may be the agent's published, checker-verified error rate against a template baseline, because it is the one place where a solo portfolio project can beat what billion-dollar vendors publish. The biggest risk is not technical: Phase 7 can easily swallow weeks of frontend polish that hiring managers weigh less than serving and monitoring. Keep three pages, cut anything that does not prove a number.

## Recommended Phase 7 scope, screens and build order

**In scope:** a read-only demo dataset (a frozen copy of the validation decisions, joined to truth, ring IDs and precomputed SHAP); three pages; deterministic reason codes; the ring drawn as of the alert; the capacity slider from a precomputed curve; the investigator agent with a checker and a published error rate; a usability test on one non-technical person (the PLAN.md gate). **Out of scope for Phase 7:** a real case workflow with logins, live re-scoring, a manager operations dashboard with simulated SLAs, money values in currency, and any public URL until the security checklist passes. Recording dispositions is optional; if added, they enter through the delayed-label door.

## Open decisions for Lubo

**1. What is one alert?** Transaction (what the code ranks today), account (what PLAN.md says), or case (a connected group of alerts, which is what vendors do). Recommendation: keep scoring per transaction, but show the queue grouped by sending account, and say so in an ADR.

**2. One capacity number.** The alert line gives about 2,370 alerts a day (5,000 over ~50.6 hours); the label simulator assumes 100 a day. Pick one for the demo story (100 a day ≈ a five-analyst team reads well) and let the slider cover the rest.

**3. Memory room.** The project already sits at its ~4 GB budget. Options: replace Ray Serve with plain FastAPI on k3s (estimated to save about 1 GB, not measured; revisits ADR 0001), run the shadow pod only during comparisons, or raise the budget.

**4. Public or private demo.** A public URL needs a box-wide change via the LuBot session. A private demo plus the video is safer and still meets the gate.

**5. Frontend stack.** Confirm what "same stack as LuBot's frontend" means (this session cannot look), and whether a static export is acceptable.

**6. The agent's model and key.** NVIDIA NIM is the plan, but its 2026 status was not checked. A new key needs a secrets-registry entry, typed by Lubo with `just secret-set`.

**7. The agent gate.** Is "zero unsupported claims" measured before or after the checker? Recommendation: report both, gate on the after number, publish the before number as the LLM's real error rate.

**8. Dispositions.** Should the Case page let a user record escalate/close? If yes, they become delayed labels through `labels.known_as_of`, never direct training input.

**9. Entity names.** The unused raw accounts file has bank and entity names that would make the queue readable. Using it means a new lakehouse table.
