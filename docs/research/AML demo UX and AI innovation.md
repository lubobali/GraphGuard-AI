# Make the demo explain itself, then argue back

**Short answer: the demo should say what it does before it shows anything, copy a real bank's case workflow instead of offering two loose buttons, and add one AI feature banks do not use yet: an AI second reviewer that argues against the human's decision using only checked facts.** Today the first screen asks "How many alerts can your team work?" before it says what the system is. Newcomers then think the model should "catch all 1,083". Investigators see "Sent on a Wednesday" as the top reason, which is a simulator artifact. Managers see nothing at all, because decisions vanish into a table nobody reads. All three problems have known fixes. For newcomers, the fixes are a one-line value statement, a three-step "how it works" strip, a "guess first" moment and a counts-based flow chart. For investigators, they are reasons grouped into behaviours, evidence before the score and a numbered money-flow picture. For managers, they are the real states (New, In review, Awaiting second review, Escalated, Closed), fixed closure codes with a short written reason, a 30-day SAR clock and a small manager view. The innovation fits the gap the industry has left open. Banks in 2026 use AI to *assist* (about 84% of AI alert reviews at one large vendor). Auto-close is about 0.5% ([Unit21](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/what-1m-live-ai-alerts-tell-us-about-ai-in-fincrime)). Second review is still done by people, after the fact. An AI reviewer that challenges each decision with facts that passed the deterministic checker can be precomputed (no live model call, near-zero cost per visit), is safe on a public site, and turns GraphGuard's existing claim checker into the main feature. The data stays synthetic, and every screen must keep saying so.

Words used below: **alert** = a payment the model put above the line for a person to check. **Disposition** = the person's decision on an alert. **SAR** = Suspicious Activity Report, the report a US bank files with FinCEN. **Four-eyes** = a second person must approve a decision. **QA** = quality assurance, a later check on a sample of closed alerts. **SHAP** = a method that splits one model score into how much each input pushed it up or down.

## Newcomers need the job, the limit and the numbers in that order

The owner's test showed one root problem: the screen started in the middle of the story. It asked a manager's question (capacity) before it said what the system is. The design research says what to do instead. Do not use long tours. In an NN/g test with 70 people, reading a tutorial did not raise task success (91% vs 94%), and it made tasks *feel harder* (ease 4.92 vs 5.49, p = 0.047) ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tutorials/)). If there is a tour, keep it to 3-4 steps and let the user start it: vendor data shows 72-74% completion at 3-4 steps and 16% at 7 steps ([Chameleon](https://chameleon.io/benchmark-report)). Google's PAIR guidebook says to explain the benefit, not the technology, and to say early what the product *cannot* do ([PAIR](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/mental-models/)).

So the top of the Queue page needs three things and nothing else. First, **one sentence that says the job**: "A computer gives each of 1,015,300 payments a risk score. A small team checks the riskiest ones first." Second, **the limit, said plainly**: "It cannot check everything, and it is not always right. It puts the most suspicious first." Third, **a three-step strip in the order of the real process**: "1 · The model scores every payment → 2 · The team checks the top of the list → 3 · Each check becomes a label the model can learn from later." The capacity slider moves to the bottom, below the list, as the manager's tool. That is where the last commit already started.

### Answer "why not catch all 1,083?" before the visitor asks it

The owner believed the model should catch every laundering payment. This is the classic screening mistake, and medicine solved how to explain it. **Natural frequencies** ("out of N, this many") are far easier than percentages. Gigerenzer reports that 10-year-olds can solve screening problems with counts but not with probabilities ([Gigerenzer, BMJ 2011](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099208_9/component/file_3562683/Gigerenzer_What_2011_VerlPDF_final_LS.pdf)). When a condition is rare, even a good test raises many false alarms per real find ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-false-positive-paradox-explains-why-you-misjudge-risk/)). Laundering here is about 1 in 1,000 payments. Lay readers misread confusion matrices, and a **flow chart** helped them most ([Shen et al., CSCW 2020](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/hongs/files/CM_CSCW2020.pdf)). Using domain words instead of "true positive" also helped ([CMU](https://dig.cmu.edu/publications/2020-confusion.html)).

The page should therefore draw one flow chart with the counts printed on it: **1,015,300 payments → 1,083 are laundering (the answer key) → the team checks the top 211 → 167 are laundering (found) and 44 are innocent (false alarms) → 916 laundering payments stay below the line (missed).** A second line shows the system's own alert line: at 5,000 checks, 542 of 1,083 are found. Then one sentence of analogy: "Like airport security. Nobody opens every bag. The scanner picks which bags a person opens, and some bags with something inside still get through." The analogy is common but untested for AML, so the test with a real person (S7.11) should check whether it works. Last, the key point the owner took longest to get: **some laundering looks exactly like normal payments.** Each hop in a ring is an ordinary amount (FINDING-003 in `docs/findings.md`). No model can rank those high without ranking thousands of innocent payments high too.

Add a **"guess first" moment** before the reveal: "About 1,083 of these payments are laundering. If your team can check 211, how many do you think you will find?" Predicting before the answer is shown improved recall in the NYT "You Draw It" studies. Low-stakes quizzes with feedback improve learning too ([Distill, Hohman et al. 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)). The same source warns that many readers never touch interactive parts. So every number must also be in plain text.

### Explain where the data comes from, once, in a small panel

The owner did not know three things. The 5M-row CSV has an "Is Laundering" answer column. The 1,083 is only the dashboard's slice, out of about 4,522 across the whole file. And train, validation and test are three windows of time. One small "Where the numbers come from" panel fixes all three. It says: IBM made fake bank data with a simulator, with the right answer included. We cut it by date. The first ~3M payments taught the model. The next 1,015,300 (the dashboard) were used to check it, and they hold 1,083 laundering payments. The last ~1M are locked away. They will be replayed once, at real speed, as the "go-live" day and the final honest score. A bank never has an answer column. That is why the demo hides it until after you decide. This panel also sets up the go-live replay as a story the visitor can return for.

### Replace the words that confused him

Each confusing word gets a plain main-text word and keeps the expert term in a one-sentence tip. NN/g warns never to put task-critical information only in a tooltip ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/tooltip-guidelines)).

| Old word on screen | Plain word in main text | One-line tip for experts |
|---|---|---|
| flags | "sent to a person to check" | alert |
| list | "the model's ranked list, riskiest first" | queue |
| rings | "a group of accounts passing money around to hide where it came from" | laundering ring / typology |
| rules / old rules | "today's fixed if-then checks banks use" | rules-based transaction monitoring |
| random picks | "checking payments at random (no system at all)" | random baseline |
| catches | "finds" (for the model), "confirms" (for the team) | true positives at k |

The capacity sentence must name the right actor. Today it says the *team* catches. It should say: "The model puts these payments at the top. When a team of about 10 checks 190 a day, they confirm the ones that are real. The model found them, the team decides." A **two-column fact box** ("Model's top 211" vs "Fixed checks' top 211", each with found / false alarms / missed as counts) reads better than a dense sentence. Fact boxes worked as well as icon arrays in a controlled study ([McDowell et al. 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/medema/v39y2019i1p41-56.html)). The chart lines get labels at their ends ("today's fixed checks", "random") and a marker at the slider's position.

## Investigators trust evidence, so reasons must point to it

### Lead with the payments, and treat the reasons as signposts

The best field study is a warning. Feedzai tested three professional fraud analysts. Showing the model score made them **faster but less accurate**: accuracy went from 62.0% to 51.0%, and agreement between analysts fell to near zero. Adding SHAP reasons won back part of the loss (59.7%) but never beat "data only" ([Jesus et al., FAccT 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.08758)). A larger follow-up found no added value from explanations over the score alone ([Amarasinghe et al., AAAI 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13503v4)). The sample is small and the domain is e-commerce fraud, not AML. But the lesson is clear. On the Case page, the transactions and the ring picture come first. Each reason is a short sentence that links to the payments it is about: "Money passed on quickly: sent 4,818 EUR 12 minutes after receiving 5,000 EUR → see payments 2 and 3." Text beats charts for most users once format is controlled ([arXiv 2408.17401](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.17401v4)). Keep waterfall plots in a "for model reviewers" drawer.

### Group reasons into behaviours and demote the Wednesday artifact

The only vendor with public docs, Google Cloud AML AI, does not show single features. It groups them into **"feature families"** written as behaviours, such as "Unusual rapid movement of funds". It gives one signed score per family and tells investigators to start with the biggest positive families ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/financial-services/anti-money-laundering/docs/understand-prediction-outputs)). It also says its score "shouldn't be interpreted directly as a probability", which matches ADR 0008's "rank, not chance". Feedzai's research interface showed the top 6 reasons in plain words, red for "pushes toward fraud" and green for "pushes toward normal" ([Jesus et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.08758)). SHAP values add up, so summing them inside a family is exact.

GraphGuard should define about six families: "Money passed on quickly", "New or rare relationship", "Unusual amount for this account", "Payment channel (partly a simulator effect)", "Account is new" and "Calendar (simulator effect)". Show 3 in the queue and up to 6 on the Case page, with "Points against" in a separate group. Weekday sits in the top 3 reasons for 77.9% of the queue, and dropping it costs nothing (PR-AUC 0.28253 vs 0.28155, FINDING-018). No vendor documents how to hide an artifact feature. So the honest order of fixes is this. First, remove weekday from the model; that is the planned first Phase 8 challenger. Until then, **keep it but demote it**: exclude it from the queue's top-3 text and show it on the Case page below a line as "Calendar effect of the simulator, not a reason to investigate", with its share of the score. Hiding it silently would make the reasons no longer add up to the score. A senior reviewer would catch that. The fix matters for trust, not only for looks. Reviews warn that SHAP displays get "absorbed at face value" ([arXiv 2309.11960](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11960)). One silly top reason can make a tester distrust all of them.

### Name the ring's shape in plain words, and say "pattern, not proof"

AMLworld's eight shapes (fan-out, fan-in, gather-scatter, scatter-gather, cycle, random, bipartite, stack) are graph names, not bank words ([Altman et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.16424)). Regulators describe behaviours instead. FinCEN describes "funnel accounts" that feed one consolidated account, and says a red flag "is not by itself evidence of criminal activity" ([FinCEN FIN-2014-A005](https://www.fincen.gov/resources/advisories/fincen-advisory-fin-2014-a005)). The FCA found that proceeds usually moved through **2 to 5 mule accounts** before cash-out ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/multi-firm-reviews/money-mules-activity-cashing-out-findings)). Each shape name should come with one sentence: fan-out = "split and send", fan-in = "collect", cycle = "round trip", and so on, plus "Synthetic data. A shape is a reason to look, not proof." Avoid "structuring" and "smurfing": AMLworld has no reporting thresholds, so those words would overclaim.

### Number the money-flow picture and explain the own-account payment

Public material on i2, Linkurious and Quantexa describes *what* their graphs show more than *how* they mark roles. Three habits can be cited. i2 labels a link with the count and total value of payments in each direction ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/node/716175)). It places payments on a timeline and lets analysts step through a sequence ([i2 docs](https://docs.i2group.com/anb/9.4.1/charts.html)). It flags accounts where "large amounts pass through without staying" ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/ibm-i2-analysts-notebook-refining-list-most-connected-items-functionality)). For GraphGuard that means four things. Put one sentence above the picture: "How the money moved in the 72 hours before this alert. Each arrow is one payment, numbered in time order. Nothing at or after the alert is shown." Add a table beside the picture with the same numbers. Use role badges in words ("Sender of this alert", "This payment was flagged"), and say "paid the sender earlier" instead of "upstream". Replace "+4 more payees" with "4 other accounts the sender paid (12,300 EUR in total), hidden to keep the picture readable. Click to show." A "Next step" button that highlights one payment at a time gives newcomers the story and costs nothing.

"Corporation #35871 → Corporation #35871" is one owner moving money between two of its own accounts at two banks (Israel Bank #24 → #37). In real banks, own-account moves are common and usually normal, but not always safe. A customer who is both sender and overseas beneficiary is a listed red flag ([PwC](https://www.pwc.com/sg/en/consulting/assets/transaction-monitoring-2019.pdf)). In this simulator, own-account payments are 18% of normal traffic and 0.2% of laundering (FINDING-005/006). The screen should mark them with an **"Own account" chip** and the sentence: "Corporation #35871 moved money between its own accounts at Israel Bank #24 and #37. In this simulator, about 1 in 5 ordinary payments look like this, and almost none are laundering." Keep these payments visible but grey, and never count them as "payees".

## Real banks run a fixed workflow with a second pair of eyes

### Use five states, fixed closure codes and a written reason

Real case tools share a small set of top-level states. Lithic's case API uses `OPEN`, `ASSIGNED`, `IN_REVIEW`, `ESCALATED`, `RESOLVED`, `CLOSED`. Its default resolution codes are `CONFIRMED_FRAUD`, `SUSPICIOUS_ACTIVITY`, `FALSE_POSITIVE`, `NO_ACTION_REQUIRED` and `ESCALATED_EXTERNAL` ([Lithic](https://docs.lithic.com/docs/transaction-monitoring-cases)). Oracle's case manager offers "No SAR Filed - Closed" and "Close and generate SAR", and **a comment is mandatory** ([Oracle ECM](https://docs.oracle.com/en/industries/financial-services/ofs-analytical-applications/enterprise-case-management/8.1.2.11.0/ecgug/taking-resolution-action.html)). Oracle Investigation Hub asks for a reason from a drop-down when escalating, and has a **"Submit and Next"** button that opens the next case ([Oracle Investigation Hub](https://docs.oracle.com/en/industries/financial-services/ofs-analytical-applications/investigation-hub-cloud/26.02.01/ihueg/escalating-case.html)). Practitioners say every disposition needs a rationale that addresses every flagged payment, and that boilerplate is a weakness ([Sardine](https://www.sardine.ai/learn/alert-disposition)). FinCEN's October 2025 FAQ says that in most cases "a short, concise statement" documenting the SAR decision will suffice ([FinCEN](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2025-10/SAR-FAQs-October-2025.pdf)).

GraphGuard's two buttons should become a small decision panel. The states are **New → In review → Awaiting second review → Escalated (SAR recommended) or Closed (no SAR)**. There are four codes: "Closed: activity explained", "Closed: false alarm (data or model)", "Escalated: needs deeper investigation" and "Escalated: SAR recommended". Each decision needs a one-line reason, with suggested phrases built from the checked facts. "Submit and next" moves to the next alert. The queue gets a status chip column, and worked alerts go grey behind a "show closed" filter.

### Make the first click final, visible and undoable by a new entry

The 15 clicks on one alert came from a missing state change. Visibility of system status is the first usability rule. For frequent, reversible actions, the guidance is "acknowledge and offer undo", not "Are you sure?" dialogs, which people learn to click through ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/)). After a click, the buttons lock at once and the panel shows "You decided: Closed, activity explained · 14:02". A "Change decision" link writes a **new** row. Lithic's activity log works the same way: append-only, with actor type, old value and new value ([Lithic](https://docs.lithic.com/docs/transaction-monitoring-cases)). The server should also treat repeat posts with the same value as one, so 15 clicks can never become 15 silent rows. The append-only `verdicts` table already fits this pattern. It needs a timeline on the Case page: who (person, AI, rule), what changed, when, and the note.

### Add the second review, the SAR clock and a QA sample

Two kinds of review exist. **Quality control** happens inside the workflow. In Oracle, an analyst's close becomes "Recommend Closure", a supervisor approves or rejects it, and a rejection returns the case to its previous status ([Oracle ECM](https://docs.oracle.com/en/industries/financial-services/ofs-analytical-applications/enterprise-case-management/8.1.2.11.0/ecgug/taking-resolution-action.html)). **Quality assurance** happens later. A second-line team samples *closed* alerts, because missed laundering can only hide there. Practitioners advise sizing samples by count, not percentage: with 0 errors in 30, you only know the error rate is probably under 10%. They also say the popular "5%" figure is convention, not regulation ([Sphinx](https://sphinxhq.com/blog-posts/aml-quality-assurance-program)). The deadline comes from US law. A SAR is due **30 calendar days after initial detection**, or up to 60 days if no suspect is known ([FinCEN FAQ](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2025-10/SAR-FAQs-October-2025.pdf)).

For the demo, the visitor plays the analyst, and the analyst's decision becomes "Awaiting second review". The second reviewer can be the AI described below, plus a clearly labelled "switch to reviewer role" for people who want to see the human side. Escalated alerts get a due date of alert time + 30 days, shown on the 2022 demo clock with "US rule; data is synthetic". After the second review, a **"Reveal the answer key"** step shows YES or NO and the ring's shape. This fits training simulators (decide, then get feedback). Gamification shows modest learning gains (g ≈ 0.49 for knowledge) in a meta-analysis ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)), and quizzes with feedback help memory ([Distill](https://distill.pub/2020/communicating-with-interactive-articles/)). Keep the score light, label it "Training mode, synthetic data", and keep the answer key hidden until after the decision. That also keeps the existing rule that truth is kept apart.

The SAR step should end in a **handoff, never a filing**. An escalated alert shows a SAR-style draft organised as who / what / when / where / why / how, built only from checked claims, and marked "Draft. Not filed. A person decides." AI-drafted SARs are held to the same narrative standard as human ones, and the bank stays accountable. One secondary source says FinCEN had issued no AI-specific guidance as of September 2026 ([Zyphe](https://www.zyphe.com/resources/blog/can-ai-draft-sar-narratives-2026)); that is not verified against FinCEN itself.

## Managers want backlog, overturns and a QA sample, not AUC

Real managers watch operations. They look at backlog by age, deadline breaches, escalation and SAR rates, reopen rates and agreement between AI and people. Practitioners call a high reopen rate in QA a named warning sign ([Sardine](https://www.sardine.ai/learn/alert-disposition)). They also advise reporting each QA error type and stratum separately, because one pass/fail number "drifts into meaninglessness" ([Sphinx](https://sphinxhq.com/blog-posts/aml-quality-assurance-program)). No independent number for alerts per analyst per day exists. The best is a vendor's illustrative 20 a day.

The demo already stores every decision. It just never shows them. A small **Manager page** fixes that, and it must work on a public site where many strangers click. So it shows two scopes: "your shift" (this browser's session) and "all visitors" (counts only, no free text shown). It has six tiles: backlog by age (0-7 / 8-14 / 15-30 / over 30 days on the demo clock, with the last tied to the SAR clock), awaiting second review, escalation rate, SAR-recommended rate, **overturn rate at second review**, and AI-reviewer vs person agreement. Below them sits a **QA sample**: a random draw plus a risk-weighted draw from *closed* alerts, checked blind against the answer key. This is GraphGuard's evaluation made operational. "Of 30 closed alerts sampled, 2 were laundering" is the below-the-line test a manager already knows. The capacity slider and the model-vs-fixed-checks fact box move here too. The chart becomes the manager's planning tool, not the newcomer's first screen.

## One innovation: an AI second reviewer that argues back with checked facts

### What banks use today, and the gap

The 2026 market has settled on "assist". FIS and Anthropic's AML agent went live at Amalgamated Bank in October 2026. It writes an analyst-ready summary in which every conclusion links to a source record, and no case closes without analyst confirmation. Its time savings are projections, not measurements ([TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/329205/20261019/fis-opens-claude-powered-aml-agent-any-us-bank-cutting-investigator-data-assembly-time.htm)). At Unit21, across more than 1M live AI alert reviews, about 84% were assist, about 16% auto-review and about 0.5% auto-close ([Unit21](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/what-1m-live-ai-alerts-tell-us-about-ai-in-fincrime)). So "every claim cites a record" is **no longer new at the frontier**. Regulators leave a vacuum. The US model-risk guidance SR 26-2 puts generative and agentic AI out of scope ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/sr2602.htm)). No US guidance says how examiners should judge agent-assisted AML decisions. Singapore's regulator names automation bias as a risk. Second review is still human. One vendor sells an AI QA agent that checks cases against the procedure step by step ([Flagright](https://www.flagright.com/ai-forensics-for-quality-assurance)), but it checks steps, not the decision, and it publishes no measured results. No published measurement of an AI reviewer that challenges a live decision was found.

### The feature

When the visitor decides, the AI acts as the second pair of eyes. It **does not say who is right**. It shows the strongest case *against* the visitor's decision, using only facts that passed the deterministic checker. If you closed the alert: "Before this closes, consider: (1) the receiver passed 96% of the money on within 40 minutes [payments 4, 5]; (2) three of the sender's five payees are accounts it had never paid before [payments 1-3]." If you escalated: "Before this goes to SAR, consider: (1) the sender has paid this receiver 14 times in two weeks [list]; (2) the amount is normal for this account." The visitor then confirms or changes the decision. Both choices are logged as new rows, and then the answer key is revealed. The manager page reports the overturn rate after the challenge, the AI-reviewer agreement rate and, on the training set, whether the challenge pointed the right way.

### Why this one

It is **genuinely new**, but built from parts banks already accept. Four-eyes review is standard, and drafts with citations are in production. An AI that challenges a human's specific decision, with every point verified by code and its hit rate published, is not used in practice. Banks avoid it live because of automation bias and accountability questions. A demo can show it safely: "the AI argues, the human decides". It also **fixes four observed problems at once**. The AI layer becomes visible on every decision instead of hiding in 128 drafts. The visitor gets feedback ("you were right / the reviewer had a point"). The workflow gains a real second review. And managers get something to measure. And it **reuses what exists**: the S7.10 agent, the tools bound to one alert, and the frozen checker that keeps only supported claims. The raw unsupported-claim rate of the drafts (FINDING-019) is the honest number to print next to it. The wording must be "0 claims that failed a data check were shown", never "0 errors". The checker checks support, not judgement, and it cannot catch claims that are true but misleading, or things the draft leaves out.

### How to run it safely on a public site under the cost cap

**Precompute everything shown by default.** Run each alert twice, once against "close" and once against "escalate". For the 128 alerts that already have drafts, that is 256 runs, made offline within the existing `llm.SPEND_CAP`. There is no live model call on page load, so visitors have no prompt-injection surface and each view costs nothing. OWASP still ranks prompt injection first in its LLM Top 10 and lists tool poisoning and weak auth in its MCP Top 10 ([OWASP MCP Top 10](https://owasp.org/www-project-mcp-top-10/)). So public free-text chat and a public MCP endpoint should stay out. If one live element is wanted later, make it a fixed menu (pick an alert, pick one of a few questions) behind per-IP and global rate limits, a server-side daily dollar cap, a cache and a kill switch. The OpenRouter console limit is a second wall. Render model text as plain text. Never give any tool the answer key. Mark the alerts that have AI material in the queue ("AI draft + reviewer" chip and filter), and say how many exist. The cost of 256 runs was not priced in this research. Compute it from S7.10's measured tokens per run before committing.

The runner-up was a "verified SAR draft with a public accuracy scorecard". It is safe and mostly built, but citations are already in production at FIS, so it is a strong basis rather than the innovation. A "what would make this alert go away" counterfactual was rejected for a public site: it reads as a guide to avoiding detection.

## Conclusion

The user test changed what the problem is. It is not missing features. It is a missing **story order**: what the system does, what it cannot do, what the team does, and what the manager sees. Each audience needs the same facts at a different depth. The surprise is that realism and clarity point the same way. Real banks' states, closure codes, second review and QA sample are exactly the structure a newcomer needs to understand "the model ranks, people decide, someone checks the checkers". The AI reviewer is where GraphGuard can do something banks do not: put a measured, code-checked AI voice *inside* the decision instead of beside it, and publish how often it was right. The go-live replay of the sealed window then becomes the honest ending: one real-speed day, decided through this workflow, scored once.

### The one innovation feature

**An AI second reviewer that argues against the human's decision using only checker-verified facts.** It is precomputed for both decisions on each covered alert, never shows the answer key, never decides, and has its agreement and overturn rates published on the Manager page. It is new because banks use AI to draft and cite, not to challenge a person's specific decision with code-checked evidence. It is safe because nothing runs live, nothing is filed and nothing closes by itself. It fits the cap because it is 256 offline runs (price to be computed from S7.10's token counts). And it makes the existing claim checker, GraphGuard's strongest asset, the thing every visitor sees.

**Gaps that remain.** No controlled study tests these explanations with AML investigators (the evidence is e-commerce fraud). No vendor publishes its closure-code list or reason templates. The airport analogy is untested for AML. No published measurement of an AI second reviewer exists, so GraphGuard's own numbers will be first-of-kind but small-sample, and must be labelled that way.
